To solve the issues of distant light precision loss, floating-point jitter, and grainy shadows in a space game, faking lighting through custom shaders and specialized architectural pipelines is an excellent choice. Standard shadow maps struggle with the massive astronomical scales of space, whereas architectural workarounds can deliver crisp, infinite-distance fidelity.
Here is a list of alternative architectures and custom shader techniques to implement fake lighting and shadows, ranked by their commonality in space flight games:

1. Distance-Field / Ray-Marched Shadows

Instead of relying on Unity's shadow maps, you bake or procedurally calculate a Signed Distance Field (SDF) for your celestial bodies or large structures. The fragment shader then ray-marches through this distance field toward the light source to determine visibility.
• Positives:
	• Infinite scaling: Shadows remain perfectly sharp and pixel-perfect regardless of camera distance.
	• No shadow map acne: Eliminates grainy shadow map artifacts entirely.
	• Soft edges: SDFs allow for cheap, mathematically perfect soft shadows (penumbra).
• Negatives:
	• Static geometry bias: Works best for static or uniformly scaling objects (like planets/asteroids); highly complex, deforming meshes require complex dynamic SDF generation.
	• Memory overhead: Storing 3D SDF volumes can consume significant VRAM.

2. Vertex-Plane and Math-Based Shadow Projection

For giant objects like planetary rings, moons, or massive space stations casting shadows on each other, you can pass the mathematical bounds (spheres, infinite planes, cylinders) directly to your shaders via a global C# script. The shader computes analytical intersection math inside the fragment function to darken pixels.
• Positives:
	• Zero VRAM cost: Everything is computed purely via math uniforms (\(LightDirection\), \(PlanetCenter\), \(PlanetRadius\)).
	• Zero pixelation: Planetary body transitions and ring shadows look completely crisp even when standing right next to them.
	• Performance: Incredibly cheap compared to texture sampling or ray-marching.
• Negatives:
	• Simple shapes only: Only works for primitive shapes or smooth approximations. You cannot easily capture the silhouette of a jagged asteroid surface this way.

3. Screen-Space Shadows & Contact Shadows (Custom Pass)

You can inject a custom Screen-Space Shadow pass into URP's Render Graph. This technique utilizes the depth buffer to trace rays across the screen towards your primary directional light (the star).
• Positives:
	• Detail at close range: Captures ultra-fine micro-details on ship hulls and control panels that standard shadow cascades miss.
	• Geometry agnostic: Works on any mesh currently visible on screen.
• Negatives:
	• Off-screen artifacts: Shadows will instantly disappear or glitch if the geometry casting them moves off-camera.
	• Distant failure: Cannot solve shadows for objects further away than the far clipping plane or those not contributing significantly to the depth buffer.

4. Hybrid Floating Origin Render Pipelines

If your distant lights are glitching due to floating-point precision loss (causing vertex jitter and shadow flickering), you can separate your game into two distinct rendering layers controlled by C# scripts. A "Skybox/Distant" camera renders planets and stars scaled down at the origin, while a "Local" camera handles your spaceship and immediate surroundings.
• Positives:
	• Fixes precision issues: Keeps lighting math within safe floating-point boundaries where Unity's native shadow maps actually work well.
	• Familiar workflow: Allows you to continue using standard URP/HDRP lighting features for your ship while manually blending the background.
• Negatives:
	• Complex asset management: Requires strict layer management and custom scripting to translate positions and light directions correctly between the two spaces.

5. Custom Vertex Shadow Volumes (Stencil Buffer)

An older but highly deterministic technique where a C# script or a geometry shader extrudes the silhouette edges of a mesh away from the light source to create a 3D volume. The shader uses the stencil buffer to mark pixels that fall inside this volume as shadowed.
• Positives:
	• Pixel-perfect boundaries: Hard shadows that do not suffer from texture resolution limits or graininess.
	• Distance independence: The shadow volume scales perfectly across vast distances.
• Negatives:
	• Fill-rate heavy: Extruding large volumes across the entire screen can heavily bottleneck GPU fill rate.
	• Hard edges only: Creating realistic soft shadows with stencil volumes is notoriously difficult and performance-intensive.
